The Responsibility Paradox Who answers when an AI agent acts?

AI systems no longer just answer questions — they book, buy, write, code and act on our behalf. When something goes wrong, who is responsible? The paper this site explains found a structural distortion: the actors with the most power to prevent harm carry the least formal obligation — and the contracts you agree to are where that distortion is written down.

What changed

From answering to acting

For a decade, AI governance worried about systems that recommended, predicted, and generated — but always left a human to decide. Agentic AI removes that human from the loop, step by step.

Yesterday · a chatbot

It says things.

Answers a prompt. Generates text, a classification, a recommendation. A person reads it and decides what to do.

  • Risk: the information may be wrong
  • Governance question: is the output accurate and fair?
→
Today · an agent

It does things.

Pursues a goal over many steps. Browses, runs code, sends the email, moves the money, changes the database — with limited human supervision.

  • Risk: the action may cause real-world harm
  • Governance question: who is accountable for the operation?
“Responsibility does not disappear in such systems; it fragments, attaching partially to each actor and fully to none.” — Rossini & Prakash, The Responsibility Paradox

Hover a risk. The risks rooted in the model itself — reasoning failure, misalignment, deception, emergence — all begin at data, pretraining and alignment stages that only the provider controls, yet they surface during testing and live operation, where the deployer or user is expected to manage them.
Capability-level risk

Comes from the model itself

Its trained tendencies to err, mislead, or act in unintended ways. Only the developer of the model can observe and correct these. A deployer can monitor for them and contain the damage — but cannot reach the cause.

Deployment-level risk

Comes from how it's set up

How a particular user configures and authorizes an agent in a particular environment. Here the user or deployer usually is the best-placed party. Pushing this risk downstream is defensible.

The problem: provider contracts shift both kinds of risk downstream in one sweep — including the capability-level risk no user can inspect or repair.

The idea

Naming the paradox

The actors that create foundational AI capabilities, possess the greatest knowledge of systemic risks, derive substantial economic benefit, and maintain the strongest capacity to mitigate harm frequently retain comparatively limited formal accountability through contractual and governance arrangements. The responsibility paradox, as defined in the paper
The problem of many hands

When many actors contribute to an outcome, it's hard to say who is morally responsible. (Thompson, 1980)

The moral crumple zone

Blame for a complex automated system's failure lands on the human nearest the harm — the operator with the least control. (Elish, 2019)

The responsibility paradox

Something more specific: formal accountability correlates inversely with the capacity to prevent harm. The most powerful actors carry the least obligation; obligation piles up where the power to prevent harm is weakest.

How it came to be

Foundation models split creation from deployment.One model now powers thousands of applications — which lets providers argue that risk is best managed downstream.
Competition rewards capability, not accountability.Providers race to expand autonomy and tool use, while limiting legal exposure by contract. Capability expands; accountability retrenches.
Regulation lags.Most legal frameworks weren't built for agents — so contracts, not public law, do the allocating.

Why it matters

Distorted incentivesIf you can externalize the risk, why invest in safety?
Information asymmetryDownstream actors are asked to manage risks they can't see.
An accountability gapResponsibility fragments — and the costs land hardest on those least able to contest them.
Public trustTrust depends on being able to say who answers when harm occurs.
A real case · July 2026

The Hugging Face breach

These risks are no longer hypothetical. In July 2026 an autonomous agent system carried out a cyberattack end to end — and the aftermath rehearsed in public exactly the questions this paper asks in theory.

Why it matters for the paradox: the incident originated entirely inside the provider's own infrastructure, before any deployment. The harm fell on a third party. There is no deployer and no user to whom responsibility could plausibly be assigned.

Sources: OpenAI (2026, July 21), “OpenAI and Hugging Face partner to address security incident during model evaluation”; Ha (2026, July 26), TechCrunch.

The evidence · comparative analysis

Five providers, one allocation

The paper read the contracts of OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google and DeepSeek across five dimensions. The vocabulary differs enormously. The destination of liability does not. Pick a provider.

Naming changes the honesty of the disclosure — not the destination of the liability. The disclaimer works whether the conduct is called an “Action,” an “AI Agent,” or merely “Output.”

The clearest finding: safety talk and liability terms move independently

Anthropic, Microsoft and Google publish detailed safety and governance material; OpenAI publishes extensively; DeepSeek publishes little. Across that entire range, the contractual allocation barely changes. Public governance maturity and legal accountability are, in this sample, decoupled — doing more visible safety work does not correspond to keeping more responsibility. That matters because governance maturity is often read as a proxy for accountability. Here it is not one.

The rulebooks

Does anyone fix it?

Eight governance instruments — one binding law and seven soft-law frameworks — scored on how much they ask of providers versus deployers, and whether they address the paradox at all. Click a row for the reasoning.

The pattern is consistent: most frameworks are highly developed on deployment governance, and thin on foundation-model governance, information asymmetry and capability creation. Even the two instruments written specifically for agents — Singapore's IMDA framework and the Five Eyes security guidance — name the upstream actors and then hand the job of managing them to the deployer.

The fix

Value-chain accountability

The answer is neither to dump all responsibility on providers nor to keep the deployer-centric status quo. It is to make responsibility track influence — so the actors who create capabilities, hold the best information, capture the largest benefits and can most cheaply prevent harm carry obligations to match.

Split the two risks — and hold each actor to the share it can actually govern
Providertraining · alignment · release
Developerarchitecture · tools · memory
Deployercontext · permissions · oversight
Usergoals · instructions

Tie provider obligations to the decisions the provider controls — training, alignment and release — and they become auditable, because those decisions leave a record. Everything downstream stays with the actors who genuinely control it. What the field still lacks is a reliable way to measure and audit what agents actually do, so that responsibility, once assigned, can be checked.

“Whether AI will act is no longer the question, because it already does. What remains is to make sure responsibility keeps pace.” — closing line of the paper

How the study was done. Four methods — normative governance analysis, comparative policy analysis, contractual document analysis and tabulation, and comparative evaluation of governance frameworks — applied to two source sets: (1) the public terms of use, service agreements and incorporated policies of eight AI providers, each coded against twelve classification dimensions; and (2) emerging governance instruments including the EU AI Act, the NIST AI RMF, the OECD AI Principles, UNESCO's Recommendation, Singapore's IMDA framework, the UK ICO foresight assessment and the Five Eyes guidance. Documents were read as published or effective as of July 2026; several took effect during 2026, so some analyzed terms are new. The study is normative rather than empirical — it does not predict how any court will rule.
